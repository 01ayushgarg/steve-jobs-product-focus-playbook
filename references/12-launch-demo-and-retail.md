# 12 · Launch, demo and the point of sale

> "Every once in a while, a revolutionary product comes along that changes everything."
> [MSW-speech-macworld-2007, 9 Jan 2007]

This chapter covers how he introduced products and where he put them in front of customers. The sources are
the iPhone introduction (2007), his private video tour of the first Apple Store (2001), what he told Stanford
students about the stores (2003) and what he told Fortune about them (2008). It is new in v2.

## What he said

**Frame the category first (2007).** He didn't start the iPhone introduction with the phone. He started with
a category, smartphones, and a problem: they were "not so smart, and they're not so easy to use."
[MSW-speech-macworld-2007] He drew it as a simple graph with two axes, smart and easy to use, put the existing
phones on it, and only then said where the new product would sit (chapter 02).

**Name three things, reveal one (2007).** He announced three products, a widescreen iPod with touch controls,
a phone and an internet device, repeated the list until the audience caught on, and then: "These are not three
separate devices; this is one device." [MSW-speech-macworld-2007] He showed a joke slide before the real
product.

**Explain what's wrong with the old way (2007).** Before showing the touch screen, he went through what was
wrong with phone keyboards: fixed plastic buttons that are the same for every application and can't change
once shipped [MSW-speech-macworld-2007]. The demo answered a problem the audience had just been shown.

**Show, don't convince (2007).** At D5 he said it's great when you can show somebody something and not have to
convince them they have the problem it solves [D5-07, 30 May 2007] (chapter 02).

**Most innovations don't fit in an ad (2003).** Apple, he said, could advertise perhaps four or five of a
dozen innovations a year; the rest had to be shown "at the point of sale" [MSW-speech-stanford-2003, 29 May
2003]. That was his reason for Apple's own stores. People using Windows, he said, wouldn't travel to try a
Mac: "We decided we had to ambush them." [MSW-speech-stanford-2003]

**Reduce the risk of walking in (2008).** To Fortune: Mac fans would drive to a special store, but Windows
users wouldn't risk a 20-minute drive for something they weren't sure they wanted. So Apple put stores in
malls and busy streets: "we reduce that risk from a 20-minute drive to 20 footsteps" [FORTUNE08 p12].

**The store as a product line you can walk through (2001).** In a private video tour of the first store, days
before it opened, he walked the layout: the whole product line at the front ("Every product we make is in
this first 25 percent of the store." [MSW-speech-apple-store-2001]), then other makers' cameras and music
players you could connect to a Mac and try, then a bar where "you could ask a Genius" [MSW-speech-apple-store-2001] for help, then a theatre.

**Launch again when the first try misses (2008).** Apple TV, he said, had been built around the wrong use. The
second version, *Apple TV, Take 2* internally, was built around what people actually wanted, movies, with a
lower price [FORTUNE08 p14].

**Earn the story before you buy one (1997).** At WWDC he argued Apple needed journalists saying it was back
more than it needed TV ads (chapter 06) [WWDC97 48:05].

## How to apply it: the launch plan (our suggestion)

1. **Write the category and its problem.** One slide, two axes if you can. Where are today's options?
2. **Write the one-line promise.** Where the new product sits on that graph, in plain words.
3. **Plan the reveal.** What's the moment the audience sees it working? Put it after the problem, not before.
4. **Demo the problem being solved.** Each demo step answers a pain you've just named. Cut any step that only
   shows a feature.
5. **Decide what's in the ad and what's at the point of sale.** Pick at most a few things for the headline.
   Everything else goes where people try the product: the store, the trial, the first-run screen.
6. **Cut the cost of trying.** What is your 20-minute drive? A sales call, a credit card, an install? Make it
   20 footsteps.
7. **Plan help in the first week.** His store had a person to ask. What's yours?
8. **Plan Take 2.** Before launch, write what you'd change if the main use turns out to be different.

## A worked scenario (fictional)

A startup launches a small business payroll product.

- **Category:** payroll tools on two axes, *takes an afternoon* to *takes a minute*, and *error-prone* to
  *always right*. Incumbents sit at afternoon and mostly right.
- **Promise:** payroll in a minute, always right.
- **Reveal:** run a real 12-person payroll on stage, timed, in 58 seconds (invented).
- **Point of sale:** the ad says only *payroll in a minute*. The tax-filing, benefits and audit features are
  shown in the free trial.
- **Cost of trying:** no card for the trial, import from the old provider in one click.
- **Help:** a live chat with a payroll specialist in the first week.
- **Take 2:** if most trials come from accountants, not owners, build the multi-client view next.

## Failure modes and limits (our reading)

- **A reveal without a problem.** The order mattered: problem, then product. A demo of features no one has
  felt the need for falls flat.
- **Over-staging.** His launches were rehearsed shows by a large company. A startup's demo can be plainer;
  the order (problem, promise, proof) is the part to keep.
- **Retail as the lesson.** The stores were his answer for a hardware company in 2001. The lesson for most
  companies is lowering the cost of trying, wherever customers try.
- **One shot.** He called Apple TV a hobby after the first version missed. Plan the second try before the
  first launch.
