# 10 · Career and life choices

> "We don't get a chance to do that many things, and every one should be really excellent." [FORTUNE08 p4,
> Feb 2008]

The 2005 Stanford address [STAN05] is the best-known source here, and this chapter uses it, but mostly in
paraphrase. It adds what he said about choosing work to Reed students in 1991, MIT business students in 1992,
the Smithsonian in 1995 and Fortune in 2008. It uses these as decision tools for work and doesn't go beyond
what he said himself about his life.

## What he said

**Choose work worth your life (2008).** Explaining what drove Apple's people, he said life is short and
everyone on the team could be doing something else, so the work "better be damn good." [FORTUNE08 p4]

**Love the work, because it's hard (1995, 2005, 2007).** At Stanford he said love of the work kept him going
after he was fired from Apple at 30, and told graduates: "the only way to do great work is to love what you
do. If you haven't found it yet, keep looking. Don't settle." [STAN05] At D5 he gave the practical reason: the
work is so hard that without love "any rational person would give up." [D5-07, 30 May 2007] In 1995 he said
about half of what separates successful entrepreneurs from the rest "is pure perseverance" [SI95], and that you
need "a problem or a wrong that you want to right" to keep going [SI95].

**Own the results (1992).** Asked by an MIT student about going into consulting, he said recommending without
owning the results and the implementation was "a fraction of the value, and a fraction of the opportunity to
learn and get better." [MIT92, spring 1992] His image: it's like a picture of a banana, accurate but "only two
dimensional." [MIT92]

**Connect the dots looking back (2005).** At Stanford he said you can't connect the dots looking forward, only
looking back, so "you have to trust that the dots will somehow connect in your future." [STAN05] His dot was
the calligraphy class (chapter 09). In 1996 he told high school graduates the same: you can't know in advance
which paths will lead anywhere [MSW-speech-palo-alto, Jun 1996].

**The mirror test (2005).** He described asking himself each morning a question about that day:

> "If today were the last day of my life, would I want to do what I am about to do today?" [STAN05]

When the answer was no "for too many days in a row, I know I need to change something." [STAN05] He said that
facing death strips away "all external expectations, all pride, all fear of embarrassment or failure"
[STAN05]. In 1995, ten years earlier, he had told the Smithsonian he tried to live each day as if it might be
his last [SI95]. A draft he emailed himself in June 2005 adds a second test: imagine looking back on your life
"in 50 years and you'll know what path is yours." [MSW-email-drafting-stanford, 9 Jun 2005]

**Bad times build character (1991).** To Reed's new students: "Character is built not in good times, but in
bad times" [MSW-speech-reed-1991, 27 Aug 1991]. At Stanford he said being fired turned out well: "The heaviness
of being successful was replaced by the lightness of being a beginner again" [STAN05].

**Life was made up (1994).** In a passage the Archive dates to 1994, he said that the world around you was
"made up by people that were no smarter than you" and that you can change it and build things others use
[MSW-one-more-thing, paraphrased after the quoted words].

**Ask for help (2003).** As a young CEO he called industry founders such as Robert Noyce and asked to buy them
lunch once a quarter to learn from them. "And everybody I ever asked said yes." [MSW-interview-leslie-berlin-2003, 24 May 2003]

## How to apply it (our suggestion)

1. **Run the mirror test for one week.** Each morning, write the main thing you're about to do, and yes or no.
2. **Count the no's in a row.** Two or three in a row is information. A full week is a decision.
3. **Name what's behind each no.** Expectations, pride, fear of embarrassment, fear of failure, or something
   real (money, obligations). Only the first four *fall away* [STAN05], in his telling.
4. **Check ownership.** Do you own the results of your work, or only advise? If only advise, what would it
   take to own one result?
5. **List your dots.** Ten things you've learned outside your job. Mark any that could connect to your work.
6. **Write the wrong you want to right.** One sentence. If you can't write it, the perseverance he described
   will be hard to find.
7. **Ask one person for lunch.** Someone who has done what you're trying to do.

## A worked scenario (fictional)

A product manager runs the mirror test for a week: no, no, yes, no, no.

- **Behind the no's:** status reports for a project she thinks should be killed (pride: she proposed it).
- **Ownership:** she recommends features; another team builds and measures them.
- **Wrong to right:** small clinics losing hours to paperwork.
- **Change:** she proposes stopping the project (chapter 01) and asks to own one clinic-facing feature end to
  end, results included.
- **Next week:** three yes days of five.

## Failure modes and limits (our reading)

- **Treating *don't settle* as permission to drift** [STAN05]. He paired it with perseverance. Love of the work was his reason
  to keep going, not to keep switching.
- **The mirror test as a mood check.** One bad day isn't a signal. He said "too many days in a row" [STAN05].
- **Dots as a plan.** Trusting that dots connect is about cheap curiosity. It isn't an argument for big bets
  on hunches.
- **Health and safety.** If the no's are about health, safety or how you're treated, talk to someone you
  trust. That's outside this playbook.
