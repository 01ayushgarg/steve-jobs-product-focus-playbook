# 09 · Technology and the liberal arts

> "I think our major contribution was in bringing a liberal arts point of view to the use of computers."
> [MSW-interview-terry-gross-1996, 1996]

## What he said

**What he meant (1996 and 1999).** To Terry Gross he gave concrete examples: fonts and typography, and graphics
good enough for photographs and artwork, for an audience that had found computers geeky
[MSW-interview-terry-gross-1996]. Underneath was a belief that "science and computer science is a liberal art."
[MSW-interview-terry-gross-1996] In 1999 he wrote that the Mac sat at the "intersection of computer science and
liberal arts" [MSW-email-apple-macintosh-fifteen, 24 Jan 1999]. His own strength, he said in the 1990s, was
that "I've always viewed technology from a liberal arts perspective" [MSW-interview-company-of-giants].

**Where it came from (1991 and 2005).** Welcoming new students to Reed College in 1991, he recalled being made
to attend humanities lectures and thinking them pointless at the time. Looking back: "It has helped me in
everything I've ever done" [MSW-speech-reed-1991, 27 Aug 1991]. At Stanford in 2005 he told the story of a
calligraphy class he dropped in on after dropping out, which had no practical use until the first Macintosh:
"It was the first computer with beautiful typography." [STAN05] In 1996 he added that the same thread led to
the LaserWriter, so the letterforms could be printed well [MSW-speech-palo-alto, Jun 1996].

**Creativity is connecting things (1996).** In Wired: "Creativity is just connecting things." [WIRED96, Feb
1996] Creative people, he said, connect experiences they've had, and can do it because they've had more
experiences or thought harder about them. Many people in the industry hadn't: "So they don't have enough dots
to connect" and end up with narrow, linear solutions [WIRED96]. His conclusion: "The broader one's
understanding of the human experience, the better design we will have." [WIRED96] He gave the same idea to
high school graduates that June: "You have to have them to connect them." [MSW-speech-palo-alto]

**Artists and engineers (1995).** "I actually think there's actually very little distinction between an
artist and a scientist or engineer of the highest calibre." [SI95] He said many of the early Mac team were
musicians and poets on the side [SI95].

**Two cultures at Pixar (1996 and 2003).** He learned there that "technology companies and content companies
have absolutely no understanding of each other" [MSW-speech-stanford-2003], and that the creative side was as
disciplined as any engineering he'd seen [MSW-speech-stanford-2003]. And the limit of technology: "no amount
of technology can turn a bad story into a good story." [MSW-interview-about-pixar, 22 Nov 1996]

**Into business (1984).** He hoped the Mac would bring that liberal-arts spirit into business. His example
was the memo, where a picture could replace pages: "you're seeing five-page memos get compressed down to a
one-page memo because there's a picture to express the key concept." [MSW-on-the-macintosh, 1984]

## How to apply it (our suggestion)

1. **Name the human job.** For your product, write what the customer is trying to express, make or
   understand, not only compute.
2. **Seat a non-engineer in the review.** Someone who judges the work as a reader, viewer or listener would.
   Give them a real vote on the experience (chapter 02).
3. **Feed the dots.** Each person on the product team brings one thing a month from outside the industry: a
   book, a building, an object, a film. Ask what it would change in the product.
4. **Test the story, not only the tech.** Before building, write the product's story for one customer in
   five sentences. If the story is weak, more technology won't fix it.
5. **Hold both sides to discipline.** Give the design and writing work deadlines and reviews as firm as the
   engineering work.

## A worked scenario (fictional)

A note-taking app has strong sync and search but low retention.

- **Human job:** people want to think on paper, not file documents.
- **Review:** a former magazine editor joins the weekly product review and flags that every new note opens
  as a blank form with five fields.
- **Dots:** one designer brings a paper notebook brand with a single ruled page and no fields.
- **Change:** new notes open as a blank page; fields appear only when you type a date or a name.
- **Result:** more notes written in week one (invented). The sync engine didn't change.

## Failure modes and limits (our reading)

- **Liberal arts as decoration.** Nice fonts on a confusing flow miss the point. His examples (typography,
  the memo) changed what people could do.
- **Taste without range.** His argument is about experience: a team with narrow experience has few dots.
  Hiring one designer doesn't fix that alone.
- **Dismissing engineering.** His claim was that the best artists and engineers are alike, not that one
  matters more.
- **The calligraphy story as a plan.** It's a story about what proved useful later, not a method for
  choosing classes (chapter 10).
