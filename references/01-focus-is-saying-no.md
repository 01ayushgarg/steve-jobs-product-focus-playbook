# 01 · Focus is saying no

> "People think focus means saying yes to the thing you've got to focus on. But that's not what it means at
> all. It means saying no to the hundred other good ideas that there are." [FORTUNE08 p6, Feb 2008]

He made the same point in 1997, 2004 and 2008, in three different settings. This chapter puts the three
together, then turns them into a method you can run on a product line, a roadmap or a single product.

## What he said, in order

**May 1997, before he ran Apple.** At Apple's developer conference a developer asked about OpenDoc, a
technology Apple had just killed. He answered in general terms. Apple's problem, as he described it, was not
weak engineers but weak engineering management: people were "going off in 18 different directions"
[WWDC97 05:15], and each project could look sensible on its own while the whole made no sense. His summary:

> "Microcosmically, they might have made sense. ... Macrocosmically, they made no sense." [WWDC97 05:49 to 05:52]

He also said what saying no costs: "When you say no, you piss off people." [WWDC97 06:09] (`[WWDC97]` is a
machine transcript of the archive.org recording, made for this repo. Each WWDC97 line used in this chapter
was checked against a second, larger-model transcription; see `SOURCES.md`.)

**September 1997, about eight to ten weeks after he came back.** In his own words he had been "back about
eight to ten weeks" when he told a small group of employees what had happened to the roadmap
[MSW-speech-apple-1997, 23 Sep 1997]:

> "We actually got rid of 70 percent of the stuff on the product road map." [MSW-speech-apple-1997]

The test he used was a customer's, not an engineer's: he couldn't make sense of the product line himself, and
"I started talking to customers, and they couldn't figure it out either." [MSW-speech-apple-1997] He kept the
remaining 30 percent, which he called the gems, and added some new work. He also described people whose
projects had just been cancelled leaving the meeting "three feet off the ground with excitement" because
they finally understood the direction [MSW-speech-apple-1997].

**July 1998, a year in.** At Macworld he explained the result. Apple had had "fifteen product platforms"
and "a zillion variants of each one" [MSW-speech-macworld-1998, Jul 1998]. He reduced the line to a grid of
two questions, consumer or pro and desktop or portable, with one product in each cell:

> "What this told us was if we had four great products, that's all we need. And as a matter of fact, if we only
> had four, we could put the A team on every single one of them." [MSW-speech-macworld-1998]

He gave two more reasons in the same passage: with only four, Apple could renew each one "every nine months
instead of every eighteen months" and could already be building the next generation while shipping the
current one [MSW-speech-macworld-1998].

**October 2004.** Asked how Apple organised for new ideas, he said innovation also comes "from saying no to
1,000 things to make sure we don't get on the wrong track or try to do too much." [BW04, 12 Oct 2004]

**February 2008.** By then, he said, Apple was "a $30 billion company, yet we've got less than 30 major
products." [FORTUNE08 p6] His clearest example of a no was the handheld organiser (PDA) Apple was pressed to
build for years. In his account, he concluded that most people only read information from a PDA on the road,
that phones would soon do that, and that the market would shrink. He added that if Apple had built one, it
wouldn't have had the resources for the iPod [FORTUNE08 p6].

> "I'm actually as proud of many of the things we haven't done as the things we have done." [FORTUNE08 p6]

(The Archive's Part III editor's note gives the 1997 cut as *seventeen to four* [MSW-part-iii-editors-note,
the Archive's words]. His own 1998 figure is fifteen product platforms. See `SOURCES.md`.)

## Why four worked, our reading

1. **People.** The best people are scarce (chapter 05). Four products means the best people are on all of
   them. This is his stated reason [MSW-speech-macworld-1998].
2. **Speed.** Fewer products, each renewed twice as often. Also his stated reason.
3. **Depth.** The next generation is already in progress when this one ships. Also his.
4. **Explainability.** A two-by-two grid is a line a customer can hold in their head. This one is **our
   reading**, drawn from his customer test in 1997.

**Our reading on the number:** four came from Apple's grid in 1998, not from a law. The rule underneath is
*as many as your A team can fully staff, and no more than a customer can explain back to you*. For Apple in
1998 that was four.

## How to apply it (our suggestion)

1. **List everything.** Products, plans, features, projects, integrations, platforms, pilots. The cut is
   only as good as the list.
2. **Find your two questions.** Pick the two questions that split your customers the way consumer or pro
   and desktop or portable split Apple's. Draw the grid.
3. **One thing per cell.** For each cell, keep the single strongest item. Leave empty cells empty.
4. **Staff the keepers.** Name an A-team owner for each one kept. If you can't, keep fewer.
5. **Merge, hide or kill the rest.** Write the decision next to each item.
6. **Test it on a customer.** Ask three customers to explain your line back to you. If they can't, cut again.
7. **Say no once, and well.** Tell each affected group clearly, explain why, and don't reopen it (see
   *Saying no with respect* below).

### If you have one product (our reading)

Most startups have one product, so *keep four products* doesn't apply as written. The same logic works one
level down:

- **Keep the core.** The core is the one experience customers come for (chapter 02). It is never on the
  cut list.
- **Cut around it.** Run steps 1 to 7 on features, customer segments, integrations, pricing plans and side
  projects instead of products. His PDA no [FORTUNE08 p6] and his pay-the-bills mistake at Pixar (below) are
  both about side work taking resources from the main thing.
- **Count A players, not items.** With five strong people, you can staff the core plus perhaps two
  supporting bets. That, not four, is your number.
- **One segment first.** In August 1997 he described Apple's plan to "really focus on two market segments
  (education and creative content)" [MSW-email-apple-entrepreneurial, 12 Aug 1997]. For a one-product
  company, the same move is picking the one or two customer segments the core is built for.

## A worked number (fictional)

A 12-person software company has one product with 31 open roadmap items: 22 features, 4 integrations, 3
pricing plans and 2 pilots for a new market. It has 4 people it would call A players.

- **Core:** the scheduling flow customers sign up for. Not on the list.
- **Grid:** *solo or team* by *self-serve or sales-assisted*. Three cells have a real customer group; one is
  empty.
- **Keep:** 1 pricing plan per occupied cell (3 to 2, merging two that differ only by seat count), 2
  integrations customers ask for weekly, 5 features that remove steps from the core flow.
- **Stop:** 17 features, 2 integrations, both pilots.
- **Result:** 31 items to 9 (a 71 percent cut, invented numbers). Four A players now own the core, the
  integrations, the plans and the five features.

**Our reading:** the share cut is a check, not a target. His 70 percent was one turnaround. If your cut is
far smaller, ask why each survivor is still there; don't cut more just to match a number.

## Saying no with respect (March 1998)

Ending the Newton upset its fans. On the day of a protest at Apple's campus, his email to employees said the
decision was "not taken lightly, and is unlikely to be reversed" and that Apple would host the protesters on
campus with a space and hot drinks [MSW-email-apple-newton, 6 Mar 1998].

**The move:** say no clearly, explain it, don't reopen it, and treat the people you disappoint well.

## Saying no to platforms (May 1997)

Asked at WWDC what Apple should do with the Newton, he started by saying his view was a minority one and
didn't decide the matter. His concern was the number of operating systems: Apple, he said, was going to have
to manage two (the Mac OS and Rhapsody), and the Newton would be a third.

> "I cannot imagine being successful trying to manage three." [WWDC97 62:12]

He added that this wasn't a judgment of the Newton itself: "I don't think that has anything to do with how
good or bad Newton is" [WWDC97 62:31].

**Our suggestion:** count the stacks, codebases or platforms you maintain. If it's more than two, treat it as
a flag worth a hard look. The threshold is ours, drawn from his remark about Apple in 1997, not a rule he
gave.

## His own focus mistake

At early Pixar, by his own account, the strategy for years was to "find a way to pay the bills"
[MSW-on-pixar-early-days, 2003] through side products. Looking back, he said Pixar would have done better to
fund the animation work alone [MSW-on-pixar-early-days]. The side businesses meant to buy time took focus
from the thing that mattered.

## Failure modes and limits (our reading)

- **Cutting the core.** A cut that removes the thing customers come for isn't focus. Protect the core
  first.
- **Cutting without staffing.** Four items with no A-team owner is just a shorter list.
- **Reopening the no.** Every reopened decision teaches the team that no means *not yet*.
- **Copying the hardware context.** His grid sorted computers in 1998. A software company's two questions
  will be different, and some markets really do need a broad line.
- **Mistaking a pause for a kill.** If you plan to come back, say so and give a date. Otherwise say no.

**Checks to run:**
1. Can a customer name your whole product line from memory? Can your newest employee?
2. If you kept only what your A players can fully staff, what would that be?
3. Which project survives only because it's interesting on its own?
4. What *pay the bills* [MSW-on-pixar-early-days] work is taking focus from the thing you actually want to build?
5. What did you say no to this year that you're proud of?
