# 01 · Focus is saying no

> "Focusing is about saying no." [WWDC97 06:02]

`[WWDC97]` is a machine transcript of the archive.org recording, made for this repo; check wording against the
video. Each quoted line was checked against the transcript, and lines with garbled audio were left out. In
WWDC97 quotes, "..." also marks a break between transcript segments.

## The diagnosis: good engineers, lousy management (May 1997)

At Apple's developer conference in May 1997, before he ran the company, a developer asked about OpenDoc, a
technology Apple had just killed. His answer was a general one:

> "But Apple suffered for several years from lousy engineering management." [WWDC97 05:02]

> "There were people that were going off in 18 different directions, ... doing arguably interesting things in
> each one of them." [WWDC97 05:15 to 05:20]

> "Microcosmically, they might have made sense. ... Macrocosmically, they made no sense." [WWDC97 05:49 to 05:52]

> "You've got to say no, no, no. ... When you say no, you piss off people." [WWDC97 06:07 to 06:09]

> "but focus is about saying no. ... And the result of that focus is going to be some really great products
> ... where the total is much greater than the sum of the parts." [WWDC97 06:57 to 07:04]

**The move:** judge each project by whether it adds up with the others, not by whether it's interesting on its
own. Being interesting in isolation is the most common reason bad projects survive.

## The cut: 70 percent of the roadmap (September 1997)

Ten weeks after he came back, he told employees what had happened to the product roadmap [MSW-speech-apple-1997,
23 Sep 1997]:

> "We actually got rid of 70 percent of the stuff on the product road map. I couldn't even figure out the damn
> product line after a few weeks." [MSW-speech-apple-1997]

> "I started talking to customers, and they couldn't figure it out either." [MSW-speech-apple-1997]

> "In addition, we've been able to focus a lot more on the 30 percent of the gems and add some new stuff in
> that is going to take us in some whole new directions." [MSW-speech-apple-1997]

**Our reading:** the test that mattered was the customer's confusion, not the engineers'. If you can't explain the line to a customer
in a sentence, you have too much of it.

The cut cancelled people's projects. He also saw what the clarity did for them: "I came out of the meeting with
people that had just gotten their projects canceled, and they were three feet off the ground with excitement
'cause they finally understood where in the heck we were going" [MSW-speech-apple-1997].

## The four products, and the A team on each (July 1998)

A year in, at Macworld, he explained the new product line [MSW-speech-macworld-1998, Jul 1998]:

> "There were the product platforms—fifteen product platforms—and a zillion variants of each one. I couldn't
> even figure this out myself." [MSW-speech-macworld-1998]

He reduced it to a grid: consumer and pro, desktop and portable. "Well, they want two kinds of products."
[MSW-speech-macworld-1998] Then:

> "What this told us was if we had four great products, that's all we need. And as a matter of fact, if we only
> had four, we could put the A team on every single one of them." [MSW-speech-macworld-1998]

> "And if we only had four, we could turn them all every nine months instead of every eighteen months. And if
> we only had four, we could be working on the next generation or two of each one as we're introducing the
> first generation." [MSW-speech-macworld-1998]

**Our reading of why four works:**
1. **People.** The best people are scarce (chapter 05). Fewer products means the best people are on all of
   them, not spread across fifteen.
2. **Speed.** Fewer products means each one is refreshed twice as often.
3. **Depth.** Fewer products means the next generation is already being built while this one ships.
4. **Explainability.** A 2x2 grid is a product line a customer can hold in their head.

(The Archive's Part III editor's note gives the cut as "seventeen to four" [MSW-part-iii-editors-note, the
Archive's words]. His own 1998 figure is fifteen product platforms. See `SOURCES.md`.)

## Saying no to customers, with respect (March 1998)

Killing the Newton upset its fans. His email to employees on the day of a protest [MSW-email-apple-newton,
6 Mar 1998]:

> "Our decision to end Newton development was not taken lightly, and is unlikely to be reversed."
> [MSW-email-apple-newton]

> "We are reserving a space for them on our campus and will provide them with coffee and other hot drinks"
> [MSW-email-apple-newton]

**The move:** say no clearly, explain it, don't reopen it, and treat the people you disappoint well.

## Saying no to markets (August 1997)

His first company-wide email listed "a decision to really focus on two market segments (education and
creative content)" among the first steps [MSW-email-apple-entrepreneurial, 12 Aug 1997].

## Saying no to platforms (May 1997)

> "I think that most companies can't be successful with one stack of system software. Rarely can they manage
> two." [WWDC97 61:16]

> "I cannot imagine being successful trying to manage three." [WWDC97 62:12]

He was careful to add that this was not a judgment of the product itself: "And I don't think that has anything
to do with how good or bad Newton is" [WWDC97 62:31].

## His own focus mistake

At early Pixar, focus came late: "Probably if you look back in the rearview mirror, we would have been better
off just funding the animation efforts and not trying to pay the bills through these other products"
[MSW-on-pixar-early-days, 2003]. The side businesses that were meant to buy time took the focus away from the
thing that mattered.

**Checks to run:**
1. Can a customer name your whole product line from memory? Can your newest employee?
2. If you kept only four products, features or projects, which four? Who is the A team on each?
3. Which project survives only because it's interesting on its own?
4. What "pay the bills" work is taking focus from the thing you actually want to build?
