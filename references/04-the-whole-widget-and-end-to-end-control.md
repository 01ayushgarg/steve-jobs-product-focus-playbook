# 04 · The whole widget, and end-to-end control

> "So the fact that Apple controls the product design from end to end, hardware, software, gives Apple an
> incredibly unique opportunity." [WWDC97 20:07]

`[WWDC97]` is a machine transcript of the archive.org recording, made for this repo; check wording against the
video. In WWDC97 quotes, "..." also marks a break between transcript segments.

## Integration is a strength only if managed right (May 1997)

> "A lot of times, both in people and in organizations, your greatest strength can be your greatest weakness."
> [WWDC97 18:42]

> "Apple has been highlighted as having an incredibly great weakness of being totally vertically integrated."
> [WWDC97 18:53]

> "I perceive it as a potential weakness if not managed right. ... I also perceive it as Apple's greatest
> strength if managed right." [WWDC97 19:24 to 19:27]

His example of why control helps was the PC industry's slow work on a shared standard: "Plug and play was an
initiative that was launched five years ago." [WWDC97 19:39] An integrated company, he argued, could solve such
problems "literally a half a decade to a decade sooner" [WWDC97 20:33].

## His own caveat: the whole widget nearly sank NeXT (1995)

In the 1995 Smithsonian interview, he said the integrated formula was a mistake at NeXT:

> "But we made a mistake which was to try to follow the same formula we did at Apple, to make the whole widget.
> But the market was changing. The industry was changing. The scale was changing." [SI95]

> "We thought we would be the last one that made it, but we were wrong. We were the first one that didn't."
> [SI95]

He added that NeXT should have "just gone on to be a software company right off the bat." [SI95] The Archive's
timeline says NeXT stopped making computers in February 1993 to focus on software [MSW-key-events, the
Archive's words].

**Our reading:** end-to-end control is a bet on scale. It worked at Apple in 1984 and again after 1997. It
failed at NeXT, where the market had moved and there wasn't the volume to pay for making everything.

## Don't invent what you can use (May 1997)

The same talk that praised integration attacked Apple's habit of building everything itself:

> "So I think the wisdom here is not to say that we've got to invent everything ourselves." [WWDC97 11:23]

> "The wisdom here is to know what 10% or 20% or 30% probably at most of the stuff we have to invent, ... and
> what we should go use that exists." [WWDC97 11:30 to 11:38]

> "We didn't invent postscript, did we? We got a laser rider [sic: LaserWriter] out of it." [WWDC97 11:41]

> "So I think this whole notion of being so proprietary in every facet of what we do has really hurt us."
> [WWDC97 11:46]

> "And yeah, it might be 10% better, but usually it ended up being about 50% worse," [WWDC97 12:08]

## Own the experience, partner for the rest (2007)

> "because Woz and I started the company based on doing the whole banana, we weren't so good at partnering with
> people." [D5-07, 30 May 2007]

> "We don't think one company can do everything. So you've got to partner with people that are really good at
> stuff." [D5-07]

His example was Google Maps on the first iPhone: Apple wrote the app and Google ran the back end. "We know how
to do the best maps client in the world, but we don't know how to do the back end so we partner with people
that know how to do the back end." [D5-07]

> "And what we want to do is be that consumer's device and that consumer's experience wrapped around all this
> information and things we can deliver to them in a wonderful user interface, in a coherent product." [D5-07]

## Why hardware and software together

He quoted the computer scientist Alan Kay twice. In 2007 at D5: "People that love software want to build their
own hardware." [D5-07] At the iPhone launch: "People who are really serious about software should make their
own hardware." [MSW-speech-macworld-2007, 9 Jan 2007] (Both are his recollections of Kay's words. The wording
differs.)

> "Because an iPod's really just software." [D5-07]

> "You can see a lot more examples of the hardware/software coupling working well." [D5-07] (About the consumer
> market, where "outside of Windows on PCs" he saw few examples of decoupled hardware and software working
> well yet. He added that nobody knew the answer for phones.)

**The move:** decide which 10 to 30 percent you must own because it *is* the experience. Buy, license or
partner for the rest. Revisit the line when the market or your scale changes.

**Use it now:** `templates/02-customer-experience-first-spec.md` (the "own, partner, buy" section).

**Checks to run:**
1. Which parts of the customer's experience do you control end to end? Which parts break because you don't?
2. What are you building yourself that you could use from someone else? Is it 10% better, or 50% worse?
3. Is your scale big enough to pay for doing the whole widget? What would NeXT's 1995 answer be?
4. Who are you bad at partnering with, and what is that costing customers?
