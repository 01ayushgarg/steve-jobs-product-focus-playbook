# 13 · The weekly product review, and small teams

> "So what we do every Monday is we review the whole business." [FORTUNE08 p9, Feb 2008]

How he ran the work week to week is less documented than his product decisions. This chapter uses what he
said to Fortune (2008), BusinessWeek (2004) and MIT students (1992), and one remark at WWDC (1997). It is new
in v2.

## What he said

**Give good people the whole picture (2008).** He said that when you hire really good people you have to give
them part of the business to run, and that he wanted them "making as good or better decisions than I would."
[FORTUNE08 p9] The way to do that, he said, was for them to know everything about the business, not only their
part [FORTUNE08 p9].

**The Monday review (2008).** Every Monday his team reviewed what had sold the week before and every product
in development, including the ones in trouble and the ones where demand outran supply [FORTUNE08 p9]. The
agenda barely changed: "80% is the same as it was the last week, and we just walk down it every single week."
[FORTUNE08 p9] He called it one of Apple's few fixed processes: "We don't have a lot of process at Apple, but
that's one of the few things we do just to all stay on the same page." [FORTUNE08 p9]

**Process makes you efficient; it isn't the source of ideas (2004).** Asked how Apple made innovation a
system, he said "The system is that there is no system." [BW04, 12 Oct 2004] He was careful to add that Apple
had great processes, but: "Process makes you more efficient." [BW04] Ideas, he said, came from people meeting
in hallways, late-night calls, and "ad hoc meetings of six people" called by someone with a new idea [BW04].

**Small teams, regular time (2004 and 2008).** In 2004 he said he had handed about half of day-to-day
management to his top executives so he could spend half his time on new products, and that he met his
operating-system team weekly "for two or three hours" [BW04]. In 2008 he said his job was to work with roughly
the top 100 people, some of them individual contributors, and to move good ideas around among them [FORTUNE08
p7].

**A small team at the top (1992).** At NeXT, he said, the top team was eight people and worked through the
important decisions until they agreed (chapter 08) [MIT92, spring 1992].

**A simple organisation (1997).** At WWDC he described Apple's restructure as a move from a company with "a
zillion P&L centers" to "a very simple organization" [WWDC97 67:23]. (Machine transcript, made for this repo;
checked against a second transcription.)

## How to apply it: the weekly review (our suggestion)

1. **Fix a slot.** One meeting, same day, every week. His was Monday.
2. **Write a standing agenda.** Last week's numbers, then every product or project in development, in the same
   order every week. Most of it shouldn't change week to week.
3. **Walk the whole list.** Every item gets a minute, even when nothing changed. Trouble items and items
   with more demand than capacity get more.
4. **Invite owners, not departments.** Each item's A-team owner (chapter 01) attends. Keep it small enough to
   argue in.
5. **Decide or assign.** Each discussion ends with a decision, or a named owner and a date.
6. **Keep ideas out of the agenda.** New ideas go to ad hoc sessions of a handful of people, called by whoever
   has the idea. Bring the result back to the review.
7. **Protect founder time for the new thing.** Block a fixed share of the week for the next product, as he did
   with the operating-system team.

## A worked number (fictional)

A 40-person company has 6 products and projects in development and three separate weekly status meetings.

- **Before:** 3 meetings, 18 attendees in total, 4.5 hours a week; nobody sees the whole picture.
- **After:** one 75-minute Monday review with 7 owners; the agenda has 9 fixed lines (sales, then each of 6
  projects, then hiring, then risks).
- **Time:** 2.5 hours a week saved across the team (invented).
- **Rule:** any line with no change gets 60 seconds. Any line in trouble gets a decision or a date.

## Failure modes and limits (our reading)

- **The review as theatre.** Status read aloud with no decisions is just a longer meeting.
- **Too many people.** His examples were small groups: eight at NeXT, six in an ad hoc meeting. A review of
  25 people can't argue.
- **Process as the product.** He said process makes you efficient, not that it creates products. Don't add
  process to fix a lack of ideas.
- **His scale, not yours.** Fortune describes a $30 billion company. A five-person startup may need a 20-minute
  review, not a long one.

**Checks to run:**
1. Is there one meeting where your owners see the whole business every week?
2. How much of your agenda is the same as last week? Is that good or a sign nothing is moving?
3. Where do new ideas get argued, and by how many people?
4. How much of the founder's week goes to the next product?
