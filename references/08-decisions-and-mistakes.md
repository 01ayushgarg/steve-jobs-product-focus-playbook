# 08 · Decisions and mistakes

> "One is, we try to differentiate between the really important decisions and the ones that we don't have to
> make." [MIT92, spring 1992]

## What he said

**Few big decisions, decided together (1992).** At MIT in 1992, asked how NeXT resolved conflict, he said he
didn't believe in telling people to *buy into* a decision they disagreed with, because sooner or later you're
paying someone to do what they think isn't right [MIT92]. Instead: "the best way is to get everybody in a room,
and talk it through until you agree." [MIT92] That meant everyone involved in the decision, not the whole
company. NeXT ran this through an eight-person team at the top, and he said there weren't many truly
important decisions: "we might have 25 really important things we have to decide on a year" [MIT92]. He could
barely recall ever overruling people as CEO; what he had done, once or twice, was tell someone who wanted to
go a different way that they were off the team [MIT92].

**Match the speed to the cost of being wrong (2003).** He described Apple and Pixar as opposite ends: at Apple,
"you make ten important decisions a day" and most wrong ones can be fixed within months; at Pixar, a few a
quarter, *very hard to change* [MSW-speech-stanford-2003, 29 May 2003].

**Decide, then fix (1997).** "Some mistakes will be made along the way. That's good, because at least some
decisions are being made along the way." [WWDC97 54:48] "And we'll find the mistakes, we'll fix them."
[WWDC97 54:55] (Machine transcript, made for this repo; checked against a second transcription.)

**Stop when it isn't working (2008).** On Toy Story, he said Pixar admitted the story wasn't good enough and
stopped production for five months [FORTUNE08 p10]. Every film since, he said, had had such a moment, and
"it's so easy to fool yourself" that something is working when you know it isn't [FORTUNE08 p10]. The iPhone
had one too: close to launch he told the team he didn't love the enclosure design, and they started over.
"And we pushed the reset button." [FORTUNE08 p10]

**Call a weak bet what it is (2008).** On Apple TV: "It's not a business yet, it's a hobby." [FORTUNE08 p14]
The first version, he said, had solved the wrong problem; what people wanted was movies, so the second try
changed course [FORTUNE08 p14].

**Ask to be challenged (1995 and 1997).** To his head of software in 1997: "Please continue to challenge me.
It's the way we get to the right decisions, and I enjoy it too." [MSW-email-avie, 25 Aug 1997] In 1995, after
Andy Grove pushed back on him: "I have changed my position 180 degrees" [MSW-email-andy-grove, 1 Oct 1995].
On management by values in 2003: agree where you're going, and the best route wins. If someone shows him
flying is faster, "I'll ditch my idea about the train." [MSW-speech-stanford-2003]

**Don't be afraid to fail (1996).** "One of the things I always tried to coach myself on was not being afraid
to fail." [MSW-interview-terry-gross-1996, 1996] He said people who get protective after failing make a mistake,
because you rarely get what you want without falling on your face a few times [MSW-interview-terry-gross-1996].

**Grade yourself in writing (1997).** At the end of 1997 he graded Pixar's leadership against milestones
written the year before, including one graded "F (but A for getting out)" [MSW-email-pixar-grades,
21 Dec 1997]. Five days later he sent the 1998 list and asked for criticism: "Please feel free to shoot full
of holes with your comments." [MSW-email-pixar-forest, 26 Dec 1997]

**Look forward (2007).** "And so let's go invent tomorrow rather than worrying about what happened
yesterday." [D5-07, 30 May 2007]

## How to apply it: the decision log (our suggestion)

1. **List the open decisions.** All of them, with an owner.
2. **Sort them.** Reversible within a few months, or hard to undo. Most will be reversible.
3. **Decide the reversible ones this week.** Pick a date to check them. Expect some to be wrong.
4. **Cap the hard ones.** Aim for a short list (his 1992 figure was about 25 a year for a whole company).
   Spend founder time here.
5. **Talk the hard ones through until you agree.** Everyone who must carry it out is in the room. If someone
   still disagrees after a real hearing, that's a team question, not a vote.
6. **Set a reset trigger.** For each big bet, write the sign that would make you stop or start over, before
   you start.
7. **Grade the year.** Write a short milestone list in January. Grade it in December. Give credit for stopping
   things that weren't working.

## A worked scenario (fictional)

A startup has 14 open decisions.

- **Reversible (11):** pricing page copy, a new onboarding email, which analytics tool, and 8 more. Decided
  in one meeting; review in 60 days.
- **Hard (3):** rewriting the backend, entering a second country, raising a round. Each gets a two-hour
  session with the people who will carry it out.
- **Reset trigger:** *If the rewrite isn't serving 10 percent of traffic by week 12, we stop and reassess*
  (invented).
- **Week 12:** it's at 3 percent. They stop, keep the parts that work, and call it a hobby until the next
  review.

## Failure modes and limits (our reading)

- **Treating every decision as hard.** Most decisions are cheap to reverse. Making them slowly is the
  costlier mistake.
- **Consensus on everything.** His *talk it through* was for the few important decisions, among the people
  involved [MIT92].
- **Sunk cost.** Toy Story and the iPhone enclosure were stopped late, at real cost. The lesson is to look for
  the moment, not to wait for it.
- **Calling everything a hobby.** *Hobby* was a label for a bet still searching for its form, with a clear
  next try. Without a next try, it's a cut.
